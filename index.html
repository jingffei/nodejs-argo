01

What CATIA is (and what it is not)

CATIA is Dassault Systèmes' high-end CAD/CAM/CAE platform. It is strongest where geometry has to survive downstream: large assemblies, complex curvature, associative drawings, and manufacturing or simulation that reuses the same model. That is why aerospace, automotive and industrial equipment teams use it.

What that means for you

  • Everything is feature history. You edit the recipe, not the result.
  • Geometry is associative. Change a sketch, and the pad, fillet, assembly position and drawing view follow.
  • Discipline pays. Clean sketches and named features make a model reusable by a colleague; sloppy ones make it unmaintainable.

Two products, one learning curve

  • CATIA V5 — the classic desktop install (CATPart, CATProduct, CATDrawing). Most tutorials, job posts and courses target V5.
  • 3DEXPERIENCE (CATIA V6 / R20xx) — roles instead of workbenches, data on the platform, largely the same modelling logic.
  • Icons and menus move between releases. When a tutorial's button is missing, check the workbench first, then the release.
Rule of thumb

Learn V5 structure first — sketches, constraints, pads/pockets, assemblies, drawings. Those concepts transfer to 3DEXPERIENCE almost unchanged; only the shell around them differs.

02

Interface & navigation

CATIA rewards muscle memory. Before modelling anything, spend 20 minutes learning to move the camera and to read the tree. If you cannot orbit without thinking, every sketch will take twice as long.

Three mouse-button navigation

ActionMouse
PanMiddle button drag
RotateMiddle + right button drag
ZoomMiddle + left button drag, or scroll wheel

A three-button mouse is effectively mandatory. On a laptop trackpad, plug one in.

The five areas that matter

  • Specification tree (left) — the live history of your model. Everything you build appears here, in order.
  • 3D viewport — where you work; the compass in the top-right snaps to planes and axes.
  • Workbench toolbars (right) — change with the active workbench, so a missing tool is usually a workbench problem.
  • Power Input field (bottom right) — type commands or feature names instead of hunting menus.
  • Status bar (bottom) — tells you what CATIA expects next; read it when a command will not complete.
Compass trick

Drag the compass onto a face or edge to align your view to it, or onto a part in an assembly to move it. Snapping the compass to a plane is the fastest way to sketch on an angled face later.

Common beginner trap

You can only create one solid body per Part Design operation. If a tool refuses to run, check that the active body is correct and that the previous feature is not still open — press Esc to leave a pending command.

05

The 6-stage modelling workflow

Almost every solid part follows the same sequence. Learn it once and CATIA stops feeling random.

  1. Choose the right support plane and sketch the profile

    Pick a base plane (XY/YZ/ZX) that matches how the part will be manufactured or how the print dimensions it. Enter Sketcher, draw the outline roughly, and keep the geometry simple — a first sketch should usually be a plain closed profile.

  2. Constrain until the sketch turns green

    Add dimensions and geometric constraints until CATIA reports the sketch as fully (ISO) constrained. Green means every degree of freedom is defined; white elements are still free to move and will bite you later.

  3. Create the first solid feature

    Leave Sketcher and apply Pad (extruded profile) or Shaft (revolved profile). This becomes the base feature the tree grows from.

  4. Add material and remove material incrementally

    Work feature by feature: pad, pocket, hole, rib, slot. Add dress-up features (fillet, chamfer, draft, shell) after the main shape is dimensionally correct, so you are not fighting fillets while changing sizes.

  5. Verify the result

    Measure critical dimensions, check mass properties against expectation, look for unintended zero-thickness walls, and — if the part belongs to an assembly — run a clash check before calling it done.

  6. Document it

    Create a drawing with the standard views, dimension it, and export a PDF. A part nobody can inspect is not finished work.

06

Sketcher essentials

Sketcher is where models are won or lost. Two habits separate people who enjoy CATIA from people who fight it: anchor the sketch to the origin, and constrain as you draw instead of drawing everything and fixing it afterwards.

The constraints you will use constantly

ConstraintWhat it doesTypical use
CoincidenceMerges two points, or a point onto a lineAnchor a corner to the origin
Horizontal / VerticalForces a line to an axis directionClean rectangular profiles
Parallel / PerpendicularRelationship between two linesSlots, brackets, ribs
TangentLine-to-arc or arc-to-arc continuityRounds blended into straight edges
ConcentricShared centre between circles/arcsBosses and bores
SymmetricMirrors geometry about a centrelineHalf-profile modelling
EqualSame length or radiusRepeated holes, hexagons
FixLocks geometry in placeOrigin point, imported outlines
Auto ConstraintDetects and applies obvious constraintsSpeed — but review what it added

Habits that pay off

  • Sketch with the origin visible and constrain to it — never let a sketch float.
  • Prefer a few strong constraints over many weak ones.
  • Use construction geometry and centrelines for symmetry, then constrain to them.
  • Cut a profile in half and mirror it when the part is symmetric; changes stay consistent.
  • Name your sketches and features the moment you create them.

When the sketch breaks

  • Over-constrained? CATIA flags the conflicting constraint — delete it rather than fighting with Fix.
  • Sketch won't close? Zoom in on corners; a 0.01 mm gap is invisible at normal zoom.
  • Elements still white? Use Sketch Analysis to list remaining degrees of freedom.
  • Profile refuses to pad? Check for self-intersection and for a stray overlapping line.

Part Design features

Solid features split into three families: add material, remove material, and dress up existing geometry. Knowing the family tells you where to look in a toolbar and, more importantly, when in the modelling sequence to use it.

FeatureFamilyNeedsNotes
PadAddClosed profileMain workhorse; supports up-to-surface and up-to-next extents
ShaftAddProfile + axisRevolved solids — pulleys, discs, flanges
RibAddProfile + centre curveSwept material along a path
Multi-PadAddMultiple profilesSeveral pads in one operation
PocketRemoveClosed profileBlind, up-to-next or up-to-surface cuts
HoleRemoveFace + positionCounterbore/countersink/threaded options; use it instead of drawing circles
GrooveRemoveProfile + axisRevolved cut — O-ring seats, circlips
SlotRemoveProfile + centre curveSwept cut
FilletDress-upEdgesApply late; keep radii consistent across a part
ChamferDress-upEdgesStandard lead-ins and deburring
DraftDress-upFace + neutral planeMould-release angles before shelling
ShellDress-upFace to openHollow out; do draft first for moulded parts
ThicknessDress-upFaceOffset a face in or out
Rectangular / Circular PatternReplicationFeature + direction/axisPrefer patterns over repeated sketches

Modelling order that survives change

Base solid first, then major cuts, then patterns, then dress-up features (fillet/chamfer/shell), and only then cosmetic details. Fillets created early break constantly when upstream dimensions change.

08

Specification tree & design intent

The tree on the left is not a file browser — it is your model's recipe, executed top to bottom. Editing history is normal work in CATIA, so build for it.

Tree operations to master

  • Double-click a feature to edit its definition; the model rolls back so you see it in context.
  • Right-click > Properties to rename, hide/show, or change colour and transparency.
  • Reorder by dragging a feature up or down when the sequence is wrong.
  • Insert marker ("Define In Work Object") to add a feature in the middle of history.
  • Deactivate a feature to test whether it is still needed — a quick way to debug a broken model.
  • Parent/Children to see what depends on a sketch before you delete it.

Design intent, concretely

  • Dimension what actually matters — to a functional face or axis, not to an arbitrary edge.
  • Keep one source of truth per size; drive repeats with patterns or formulas.
  • Model symmetric parts symmetrically so a change flips both sides.
  • Leave the model in a state where a stranger can find and change one dimension in under a minute.
09

Assembly design

An assembly is a .CATProduct that references parts. It contains no geometry of its own — only components and the constraints that position them. That is why assemblies stay small and why broken links to parts are the most common problem you will hit.

Sequence that avoids pain

  1. Create a new CATProduct, then insert existing components (do not copy geometry in).
  2. Fix the base component — the one everything else references.
  3. Add constraints one at a time, and rotate the view after each to confirm the move.
  4. Watch the update symbol; if a constraint cannot be satisfied, undo instead of stacking more.
  5. Finish with a clash and a section analysis.

Constraints & tools you need

  • Coincidence — align axes, points and planes.
  • Contact — faces touch without a gap.
  • Offset — a controlled gap between faces.
  • Angle — fixed relative rotation.
  • Fix — perfect but inflexible; use it only on the base part.
  • Manipulation — drag parts to test remaining freedom before constraining.
  • Clash / Section — find interference and inspect internal clearances.
  • Scenes & exploded views — for reviews and documentation.
Moving or copying parts

If an assembly is opened on another machine or from another folder, CATIA may not find the parts. Use File > Save Management to see and repair every link, and keep a project folder structure you can copy whole.

10

Drafting & drawings

Drafting turns the 3D model into the 2D document a workshop actually uses. Because it is associative, the drawing is a view of the model — never redraw geometry by hand.

  1. Start from the model, not a blank sheet

    Create a new CATDrawing from the part or assembly, choose a standard (ISO, ANSI, JIS) and a sheet size, then place the front view.

  2. Project the standard views

    Use Projection > Views to generate top, side and isometric views from the front view. Keep the projection type consistent across the whole drawing.

  3. Add the views that explain the part

    Section (cut), section cut, detail (zoom), broken and auxiliary views. Use the minimum that communicates the geometry — drawings die from clutter, not from missing views.

  4. Dimension and annotate

    Generate dimensions intelligently, then clean them up: move them off the geometry, align them, and delete duplicates. Add datums, GD&T, surface finish and notes as required by your standard.

  5. Title block, then export

    Fill in the title block (part number, material, scale, revision, author), then export to PDF or DXF. Check that the printed scale matches the stated scale.

Associativity test

Change a dimension in the 3D part, return to the drawing and press Update. If views and dimensions follow, your drawing is correctly linked. If they do not, you generated a view onto geometry that is no longer referenced.

11

Surfacing basics (when solids are not enough)

Complex blends, aerodynamic skins and class-A shapes are built as surfaces first, then turned into solids. You do not need surfacing on day one, but knowing the vocabulary removes the fear.

Wireframe

Points, lines, splines, circles and the projections/intersections between them. Surfaces are only as good as the curves they are built from.

Surfaces

Extrude, revolve, offset, sweep, multi-sections surface (loft), blend, fill and join. Work in Generative Shape Design for the modern tool set.

Turning surfaces into solids

Thicken, close a joined surface into a volume, then Add it into a PartBody, or use Close Surface and Sew Surface.

Discipline required

Surface models tolerate nothing: a 0.001 mm gap or a mismatched tangent makes a join fail. Build curves first, check continuity, and join surfaces as you go instead of at the very end.

Workspace habits

  • Save before every risky edit; CATIA has no auto-recovery worth relying on.
  • Work in project folders with relative structure so assemblies keep their links.
  • Export STEP (.stp) for interchange — not CATIA-native files.
  • Keep a scratch CATPart for testing features instead of experimenting on real work.

Debugging a broken model

  • Press Ctrl+U; the update error usually names the failing feature.
  • Right-click the suspect feature > Parent/Children to see what depends on it.
  • Deactivate the feature to confirm it is the cause.
  • Roll the insert marker back and step forward one feature at a time.

Files, settings & where to get help

File types you will meet

.CATPartSingle part
.CATProductAssembly of parts and sub-assemblies
.CATDrawing2D drawing linked to a part or product
.CATProcessManufacturing process document
.stp / .stepNeutral 3D interchange — use this to send models out
.igs / .igesOlder neutral interchange, surface-oriented
.stlTessellated mesh for 3D printing
.dxf2D exchange for laser/plasma cutting

Settings worth changing early

  • Units — pick mm or inch once, at the start of a project.
  • Working directory & autosave — Tools > Options > General; point them at a folder you back up.
  • Display quality — raise it for final screenshots, not for modelling, or the viewport crawls.
  • Save a drawing template with your standard sheet and title block.
  • Set the drafting standard (ISO/ANSI/JIS) before producing drawings, not after.

When you get stuck

  1. Read the status bar — it usually states exactly what CATIA is waiting for.
  2. Search the exact error text plus your release year; that solves most dead ends.
  3. Ask a specific question with a screenshot of the tree and the failing feature.
  4. For authoritative answers, use the official documentation shipped with your install (F1 in most dialogs) and the Dassault Systèmes user community.
The one exercise that beats everything else

Model a part you physically own. Measure it with calipers, build it, then make the drawing. Nothing else forces you to deal with real tolerances, real design intent and real "why won't this fillet work".
